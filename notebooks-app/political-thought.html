Getting Oriented

Background

Why the course starts with Plato

POSC 260's throughline is a single question asked across two thousand years: what grounds political order — human nature, divine will, or human construction — and who's entitled to rule on that basis? The syllabus is organized so each later thinker is legible as an answer to, or a rebellion against, the one before: Aristotle largely accepts Plato's premise that politics should aim at virtue but rejects his methodology (empirical observation of existing constitutions vs. Plato's top-down design from first principles); Augustine reframes the whole project through Christian revelation; Machiavelli and Hobbes represent the sharpest possible break, grounding politics in human nature stripped of any appeal to a transcendent Good. Reading Plato first isn't just chronological — it's the premise everyone after him is reacting to.

Historical context

The Republic was written in the early 4th century BCE, in the aftermath of Athens's defeat in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE) and the brutal, short-lived oligarchic coup known as the Thirty Tyrants (404–403 BCE) — a regime that included Plato's own relatives and that executed or exiled thousands of Athenians. Democracy was restored in 403 BCE, and in 399 BCE that restored democracy tried and executed Plato's teacher, Socrates, on charges of impiety and corrupting the youth. This isn't background trivia: Plato lived through both a violent oligarchy and a democracy that killed his teacher, which is a large part of why the Republic treats both as failed regimes (Book VIII) and searches for a third alternative grounded in knowledge rather than either wealth or popular opinion.

The Socratic method

Nearly everything in the Republic happens through elenchus — Socrates cross-examining an interlocutor's claim by drawing out its logical consequences until it contradicts itself or collapses. It's worth tracking this as a method distinct from the content of any given argument: Adeimantus's "checkers game" complaint in Book VI (that people walk away out-argued but not persuaded) is really a critique of elenchus itself, and it's a useful lens for the whole dialogue — ask not just "what is Socrates arguing?" but "how is he getting his interlocutors to agree, and does that agreement actually track truth?"

Week 1 · Book I

Fri 8/28Republic, Book I

Socrates narrates a visit to Piraeus, where he's drawn into a conversation about justice at the house of Cephalus, a wealthy elderly metic. The book moves through three increasingly serious attempts to define justice, each one refuted. Cephalus opens with a practical, old-man's view: justice is honesty and paying what you owe. Socrates breaks this with a single counterexample — returning a weapon to a friend who's gone mad would be harmful, not just — showing that a rule-based definition ("always repay debts") can't survive contact with edge cases. Cephalus's son Polemarchus revises this into "helping friends, harming enemies," which Socrates dismantles on two fronts: we're often wrong about who our real friends are, and more damningly, justice is a virtue, so if harming someone makes them worse (less virtuous), a just person harming anyone at all is a contradiction. Then Thrasymachus arrives with the book's real challenge: justice is nothing but "the advantage of the stronger" — rulers make laws that serve themselves, and obedience is just submission dressed up as virtue. Socrates counters with a craft analogy (medicine aims at the patient's good, not the doctor's) and a functional analogy (a soul divided against itself, like a band of thieves with no honor among them, can't act effectively even at injustice) — but notably, the book ends without a positive definition of justice, only refutations. That's deliberate: it sets up Glaucon's demand in Book II for something better than a knockout argument.

Elenchus: the Socratic method of cross-examination — testing a claim by drawing out its consequences until it contradicts itself.
"Advantage of the stronger": Thrasymachus's claim that justice is really just whatever serves the interests of whoever holds power.
Craft analogy: Socrates's move of comparing ruling to a craft (medicine, navigation) that, properly practiced, aims at the good of what it governs rather than the practitioner's own advantage.

Before class, be able to answer: Why does Socrates think Thrasymachus's "obedience serves the ruler's advantage" claim breaks down once you admit rulers can be mistaken about their own interest?

Week 2 · Book I (cont.), II, III–IV

Tue 9/1Republic, Book I (cont.); Book II

Book II opens with Glaucon dissatisfied: Thrasymachus was silenced, not actually refuted, and a real defense of justice needs to survive its strongest form. He sorts goods into three categories — valued only for consequences, valued only in themselves, and valued both ways — and demands Socrates show justice belongs in that third, best category, not merely tolerated for its reputation. He then builds the strongest possible case against justice being good in itself. First, a social-contract account: people agree to be just only because they lack the power to get away with injustice, illustrated by the Ring of Gyges — if a person could turn invisible and act with total impunity, would they still choose to be just? Second, a thought experiment: strip away reputation entirely and compare a perfectly unjust man with a flawless reputation for justice against a perfectly just man falsely believed to be unjust and punished for it — which life is actually better? Adeimantus adds that even parents and poets only praise justice for its payoffs (reputation, divine reward), never as good in itself — which is exactly the gap Socrates has to close. Socrates's strategy: since justice is hard to see clearly in one person, examine it "writ large" in a city first, and starts building one from basic needs upward.

Ring of Gyges: a ring granting invisibility, used to test whether justice is chosen for its own sake or only out of fear of getting caught.
The three kinds of goods: Glaucon's typology — goods good only for consequences, only in themselves, or both; justice needs to land in the third.

Before class, be able to answer: What is Glaucon actually trying to accomplish by making the strongest possible case against justice, if he doesn't personally believe it?

Wed 9/2, 6:30pmRepublic, Books III–IV

Book III finishes designing the guardians' early education, centered on poetry and music before physical training. Socrates argues stories shape character through imitation, so tales of gods lying, quarreling, or an afterlife to be feared get censored or rewritten — future guardians need role models of virtue and courage, not reasons to fear death in battle. Musical modes and rhythms are similarly filtered to cultivate courage and moderation rather than softness. Physical training is paired with this, not separated from it: gymnastics alone, Socrates warns, produces harshness without the arts to civilize it. The book closes with the "noble lie" — a founding myth telling citizens they're born with gold, silver, or bronze/iron in their souls, meant to secure acceptance of the city's class structure. Book IV completes the city's three classes — rulers, auxiliaries, producers — and maps the four cardinal virtues onto it: wisdom in the rulers' knowledge, courage in the auxiliaries' training, moderation as citywide agreement about who should rule, and justice as each class doing only its own job. Socrates then argues the same tripartite structure exists in a single soul — reason, spirit, appetite — with justice meaning each part plays its proper role and reason governs. This city/soul parallel is the structural backbone for everything through Book IX.

Noble lie: a founding myth (the "myth of metals") told to unify the city around its class structure.
Tripartite soul: Socrates's division of the soul into reason, spirit (thumos), and appetite.
Justice (city/soul definition): each part/class doing its own proper job without interfering in the others'.

Before class, be able to answer: Explain the city/soul analogy in one or two sentences — how does each of the three soul-parts map onto a class in the city?

Week 3 · Books V; VI

Tue 9/8Republic, Book V

Socrates works through three "waves" of increasingly provocative claims: equal education and roles for women guardians, communal wives/children/property among the guardian class (meant to eliminate private interest that could divide the ruling class), and — the biggest wave, the one the rest of the unit hinges on — that philosophers must rule, or existing rulers must become philosophers, before the ideal city can exist at all. Socrates is explicit that this claim will draw "waves of laughter," which is itself a signal of how far it departs from Athenian common sense about who's fit to govern.

The three waves: Socrates's three escalating, deliberately provocative proposals in Book V.
Philosopher-king thesis: the claim that political power and philosophical wisdom must converge in the same person/people.
“Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, and political greatness and wisdom meet in one, and those commoner natures who pursue either to the exclusion of the other are compelled to stand aside, cities will never have rest from their evils — nor the human race, as I believe — and then only will this our state have a possibility of life and behold the light of day.”Republic 473c–e (Jowett trans., public domain) — your course edition is Bloom's, so exact wording/pagination will differ.

Before class, be able to answer: Why does the "compelled to stand aside" clause matter as much as the philosopher-ruler claim itself — what's the argument doing on both ends?

Thu 9/10Republic, Book VI

Book VI defines the philosopher and defends the claim Book V just dropped. A true philosopher loves wisdom as a whole — the eternal, unchanging Forms — not just particular beautiful or pleasant things (contrasted with mere "sight-lovers" and "sound-lovers"). This orientation toward truth brings a cluster of virtues along with it almost automatically: honesty, courage, moderation, justice. Adeimantus interrupts with an objection: no one can refute Socrates step by step, yet people walk away out-argued rather than genuinely persuaded — like a novice checkers player boxed in by an expert who can't say exactly where they went wrong — and real "philosophers," he says, tend to be useless or corrupt in practice. Socrates doesn't dismiss this; he agrees it's often true, but blames the city, not philosophy itself, via the Ship of State analogy: a true navigator is dismissed as a useless "stargazer" by a crew that flatters the shipowner and fights over the wheel — talented natures get corrupted by public opinion and political life instead of properly educated. The book then climbs toward the philosopher's highest object of knowledge: the Form of the Good, explained through the Sun analogy (the Good illuminates truth the way the sun illuminates sight, and is the source of intelligibility itself, not just one more object of knowledge) and the Divided Line, which maps four ascending levels of cognition.

Ship of State: Socrates's analogy for why a true philosopher looks useless — the city mistreats and misuses him, rather than philosophy itself producing uselessness.
Form of the Good: the highest Form, source of truth and intelligibility for everything else that can be known.
Divided Line: four ascending levels of cognition — imagination (eikasia), belief (pistis), thought (dianoia), understanding (noesis).
Why are philosophers entitled to rule, according to Socrates's argument here?
Because ruling is treated as a craft that should belong to whoever actually knows the good of what's governed — not to whoever is popular or powerful. Non-philosophers are stuck judging by opinion and appearance (the Divided Line's lower levels); only someone who has grasped the Form of the Good can reliably tell what's truly good for the city as opposed to what merely seems good. Just as importantly, philosophers don't want power, which is precisely why they can be trusted with it — unlike rulers who chase power for its own sake and rule badly as a result. It mirrors the city/soul parallel from Book IV: reason should govern spirit and appetite in the soul, so philosophers (reason's representatives) should govern the city.
What is Adeimantus's "checkers game" objection actually targeting?
Not the truth of Socrates's conclusion, but the persuasiveness of Socratic argument itself — you can win every individual exchange (elenchus) and still leave the listener's real intuitions unconvinced, the way a skilled player can box in a novice without the novice understanding why. It's Plato flagging the gap between logical victory and genuine persuasion, and it's what forces Socrates to answer with the Ship of State analogy — an appeal to how philosophers actually get treated — rather than another logical proof.

Week 4 · Book VII; VIII–IX

Tue 9/15Republic, Book VII

Opens with the Allegory of the Cave: prisoners chained since childhood face a wall, mistaking shadows cast by a fire for the whole of reality. One is freed, painfully dragged up into daylight, and gradually comes to see the sun itself — the Good — as the source of everything visible. Returning to free the others, his eyes fumble in the unfamiliar dark; the prisoners conclude the ascent ruined him and may turn violent against anyone who tries to free them. Socrates maps this directly onto Book VI's imagery (cave = the visible world, ascent = the soul's education, sun = the Good) and reframes education as turning the soul to face the right direction, not pouring knowledge into an empty mind. The rest of the book lays out the actual curriculum meant to accomplish that turn: arithmetic, plane and solid geometry, astronomy, and harmonics, all studied as abstract reasoning rather than empirical observation, culminating in dialectic — the only method capable of moving past hypotheses to grasp the Good itself as an unconditioned first principle. Socrates lays out an age-based timeline stretching into a ruler's 50s, and reiterates that philosophers must be compelled to rule, since they'd rather stay contemplating the Forms — a reluctance he treats as exactly what makes them trustworthy with power.

Allegory of the Cave: the image of prisoners mistaking shadows for reality, used to illustrate the soul's ascent from ignorance to knowledge of the Good.
Turning the soul (periagoge): Plato's model of education as redirection, not the transfer of information into an empty mind.
Dialectic: the capstone discipline of the curriculum, reasoning that moves beyond hypotheses to the unconditioned first principle (the Good).

Before class, be able to answer: Why does Plato insist the guardians' math and astronomy be studied abstractly rather than practically or observationally — what's that restriction doing for the soul's ascent?

Thu 9/17Republic, Books VIII–IX

Book VIII traces the decline of political regimes away from the ideal aristocracy, through timocracy (rule by honor-lovers, prizing military glory over wisdom), oligarchy (rule by the wealthy, where money replaces honor as the object of desire), democracy (rule by the many, prizing freedom to the point of having no stable hierarchy of values at all), down to tyranny — each regime paired with a corresponding character type in the individual soul, so the city's decline and the soul's decline are the same story told twice. Book IX completes this with the tyrannical man as the most unjust and, Socrates argues, the most miserable type of all — enslaved to his own worst appetites and living in constant fear. Socrates offers three separate proofs that the just life is the happiest life, including the striking image of the soul as a composite creature — a human, a lion, and a many-headed beast fused together — where justice means the human (rational) part governs the other two, and injustice means the beast is left to run the whole creature.

Regime decline sequence: aristocracy → timocracy → oligarchy → democracy → tyranny.
Soul/regime parallel: each political regime in Book VIII corresponds to a dominant character type in the individual soul.
The composite-creature image: the soul pictured as a human, lion, and many-headed beast in one body, used in Book IX to argue justice means reason governing the other two.

Before class, be able to answer: Pick one regime from the decline sequence and explain what value it prizes above the others, and how that value's overreach produces the next regime down.
