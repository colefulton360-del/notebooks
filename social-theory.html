Getting Oriented

Mon 8/31Morrison, “Introduction” to Formations of Modern Social Thought, pp. 1–6

This sets up why the course begins with a “why now” question instead of jumping straight to Marx. Morrison argues social theory emerged as a discipline because European societies in the late 1700s–1800s were going through three ruptures at once — political, economic, and a redefinition of the individual — and thinkers needed a new kind of explanatory tool to make sense of change at that scale. The reading frames “social theory” as a genre: large-scale, comparative-historical explanations that look across societies and eras for patterns and causes, not a description of one event. Read it slowly — the three shifts introduced here are the scaffolding the entire semester hangs on. Marx, Durkheim, and Weber are best understood as three different attempts to explain the same rupture.

Meta-theory / large-scale explanatory perspective: a framework built to explain broad patterns across whole societies and historical periods, not a single case.
Comparative-historical method: studying change by comparing different societies and time periods side by side to isolate causes.

Before class, be able to answer: Name the three developments of modernity this reading introduces, and explain in one sentence what’s changing in each.

Wed 9/2Morrison, pp. 7–end

Finishes the intellectual groundwork by turning to the Enlightenment as the philosophical shift underneath the political and economic ones. Before it, claims about truth and social order leaned on religious revelation and inherited tradition — a king rules because God ordained it, a peasant stays a peasant because that’s the order of things. The Enlightenment swapped that for reason, observation, and skepticism as the tests for what’s true, which is part of what makes it possible to even ask “why is society organized this way?” instead of just accepting that it is. Morrison also flags evolutionism — the belief that history moves in a single line of progress — as a powerful, double-edged Enlightenment-era idea: it fueled optimism about social improvement, but it was also weaponized into racist hierarchies of “advanced” vs. “primitive” peoples, a thread that returns directly in the Du Bois unit. By the end you should be able to hold Marx, Durkheim, and Weber’s projects in your head as different responses to the same Enlightenment-era assumptions.

Enlightenment: the 18th-century intellectual movement that replaced tradition/religious authority with reason and empirical observation as the basis for truth claims.
Evolutionism: the (contested) belief that societies develop in a single line from “primitive” to “advanced.”

Before class, be able to answer: Why does Morrison treat the Enlightenment as a precondition for social theory existing at all, not just background color?

Unit 1 · Marx

Fri 9/4Dillon Ch. 1: Key Concepts through Material and Social Existence Intertwined

This is Marx’s opening theoretical claim, and everything else in the chapter builds on it. Marx argues material conditions — how a society organizes the production of food, shelter, and goods — determine social existence (our relationships, institutions, even our ideas), not the reverse. This directly inverts Hegel, for whom historical change was driven by the unfolding of ideas. Marx’s version is often summarized as “it’s not consciousness that determines life, but life that determines consciousness.” Practically: if you want to explain why a society has the family structures, laws, or dominant beliefs it has, Marx says start by looking at how that society produces its livelihood — who owns what, who works for whom — not at the ideas people happen to hold. Keep the identity-box exercise in mind: it’s meant to get you thinking concretely about how material objects in your own life shape your social identity — the same logic, just scaled down from a whole society to one person.

Material existence: the concrete economic/productive conditions of a society — how goods and livelihoods get produced.
Social existence: the relationships, institutions, and consciousness Marx argues are shaped by material existence.
Dialectical materialism: Marx’s method — historical change driven by material/economic contradictions, not the unfolding of ideas.

Before class, be able to answer: In your own words, would Marx explain a society’s laws by starting from ideas or starting from production? Why?

Mon 9/7Dillon Ch. 1: Capitalism as a Distinctive Social Form through Exchange Value/Use Value gap

Marx pauses on a question that sounds almost too basic: what actually makes capitalism different from feudalism or slavery? His answer: capitalism is organized around the ceaseless accumulation of capital for its own sake — production isn’t ultimately about meeting needs, it’s about generating profit that gets reinvested to generate more profit, in an open-ended cycle. Critically, the relationship between capitalist and worker is purely economic: no customary, religious, or personal obligation binds them the way a feudal lord was (at least nominally) obligated to his serfs. This section also introduces use-value (what a thing is actually useful for) vs. exchange-value (what it can be sold for) — a distinction that looks small here but becomes the engine of the commodity fetishism argument later in the chapter, so lock it in now.

Use-value: what a good is actually useful for (a coat keeps you warm).
Exchange-value: what a good can be traded or sold for in the market (a coat’s price tag).
Ceaseless accumulation: capitalism’s defining drive to reinvest profit into more profit, with no natural stopping point.

Before class, be able to answer: Why does Marx say the capitalist/worker relationship has “no non-economic ties,” and why does that matter for defining capitalism as historically distinctive?

Wed 9/9Dillon Ch. 1: Division of Labor and Alienation through end of Marx chapter

Probably the single most testable reading of the unit. Start from the division of labor: under capitalism, production gets broken into narrow, specialized tasks, so no one worker makes a whole product start to finish. Marx argues this fragmentation produces alienation — estrangement — along four distinct axes: workers become alienated from the products they make (owned and sold by someone else), from the act of production itself (labor becomes forced and exhausting rather than self-expression), from their own “species being” (the free, creative capacity Marx thinks defines humanity), and from other workers (competing for jobs and wages instead of cooperating). Have one concrete, specific example ready for each of the four types before class — you’ll likely be asked to apply this, not just define it.

Alienation: an unnatural, broken relationship between things that should be connected — worker/product, worker/labor, worker/self, worker/other workers.
Species being: for Marx, the free, creative capacity to imagine and make things (not just survive) that he sees as distinctly human.

Before class, be able to answer: Pick one of the four types of alienation and describe a concrete, modern example of it.

Fri 9/11Lemert: Marx & Engels, “The Manifesto of Class Struggle” excerpt

This primary-source excerpt puts Marx and Engels’s most quoted line directly in front of you: “the history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggles.” Read it as the political payoff of 9/4–9/9 — if material conditions and class position shape consciousness, then history itself, for Marx, is the story of two classes locked in an ongoing struggle over who controls the means of production: the bourgeoisie (owners) and the proletariat (wage-workers). What sets this excerpt apart from the more analytical Dillon chapter is its tone — a call to recognition and action, not just description — and its argument that industrialization is actively sharpening this conflict by concentrating workers together and stripping away the messier class distinctions of earlier eras (guild masters, small landowners) into one central antagonism.

Bourgeoisie: the class that owns the means of production (factories, capital).
Proletariat: the class that must sell its labor for wages to survive.

Before class, be able to answer: According to Marx and Engels, why does industrial capitalism simplify class conflict into two camps rather than many?

Mon 9/14Marx application: 3 NYT-style articles (wages/wealth, AI & productivity, cost of being poor)

Reading notes due before class. Is the AI/productivity piece about alienation (technology further fragmenting or replacing human labor)? Is the cost-of-poverty article about false consciousness (systemic causes read as individual failure)? A strong application names the mechanism, not just the theme.

Unit 2 · Durkheim

Wed 9/16Dillon Ch. 2: Key Concepts through Methodological Rules

Durkheim opens by defining sociology’s actual object of study: social facts — ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that exist outside any one individual, are spread generally across a society, and exert a real, coercive pull on behavior whether or not any given person notices it. Language is the textbook example: you didn’t invent English, it existed before you and shapes what you can even think and say, yet it doesn’t feel like an external force pressing on you day to day. The methodological rule that follows was genuinely radical for its time: study social facts as things — objectively, through observation and comparison, the way a natural scientist studies matter — and explain a social fact only by other social facts, never by an individual’s private psychology. This is Durkheim’s founding gesture toward functionalism, and it puts him in direct tension with Marx’s more conflict-driven method — Durkheim is much more interested in what keeps a society functioning than in what’s tearing it apart.

Social fact: a way of acting/thinking/feeling that is external to any one individual, widespread across society, and coercive.
Positivism: studying social life objectively, through observation and classification, the way natural sciences study the physical world.
“Explain social facts with social facts”: Durkheim’s rule that the cause of a social pattern must be another social pattern, not individual psychology.

Before class, be able to answer: Why would Durkheim say a psychological explanation for suicide rates (“sad people kill themselves”) is a methodological mistake?

Fri 9/18Dillon Ch. 2: The Nature of Society through Modern Society

With ground rules set, Durkheim asks the question the unit is really about: what holds a society together, and does that change as societies modernize? In small, homogeneous, traditional societies, people are held together by mechanical solidarity — a shared collective conscience, meaning nearly everyone believes the same things and does similar work, so cohesion comes from sameness. As societies industrialize and the division of labor deepens, Durkheim argues a different bond takes over: organic solidarity, where people are held together precisely because they’re different from each other and depend on one another’s specialized functions — the way organs in a body depend on each other despite doing very different jobs. Notice this is effectively a rebuttal of the idea that specialization is only alienating (Marx’s reading from 9/9): for Durkheim, the same division of labor can also be what makes a complex modern society cohere at all.

Mechanical solidarity: social cohesion based on shared beliefs and similarity, typical of small traditional societies.
Organic solidarity: social cohesion based on interdependence between differentiated, specialized roles, typical of modern societies.
Collective conscience: the shared set of beliefs and sentiments common to members of a society.

Before class, be able to answer: Contrast how Durkheim and Marx would each evaluate the division of labor — a source of cohesion, or a source of alienation — and why.

Actual guided questions for this class:
“The totality of beliefs and sentiments common to the average members of a society forms a determinate system with a life of its own. It can be termed the collective or common consciousness [conscience] … By definition it is diffused over society as a whole … it is independent of the particular conditions in which individuals find themselves. Individuals pass on, but it abides … [and] links successive generations to one another.”Division of Labor in Society 38–39, cited in Dillon 88
Pick a passage from “The Nature of Society” (pp. 83–84) — what is Durkheim explaining, and what’s the role of a society’s moral system?
Generally, that society is a reality sui generis — a thing in its own right, not just the sum of individuals — that exercises real, external influence over its members. The exact point depends on the paragraph picked: society constraining behavior through shared norms, the collective’s moral authority over the individual, or individuals being formed by society rather than the reverse. A society’s moral system, for Durkheim, is functional: it regulates individual behavior, binds people to the group, and creates the shared meaning that makes coordinated social life possible.
What central concept is being described in the quoted passage above?
The collective conscience — the shared totality of beliefs/sentiments common across a society, with “a life of its own,” diffused across society (not located in any one person), and persisting across generations. A textbook social fact, and the foundation of mechanical solidarity.
What does Durkheim mean by solidarity, and how does it differ in traditional vs. modern societies?
The bond of cohesion holding a society together. Traditional societies: mechanical solidarity — held together by likeness (shared beliefs/work), repressive law for violations. Modern societies: organic solidarity — held together by interdependence between differentiated, specialized roles, restitutive law instead. His answer to “what holds modern society together without one shared belief system”: interdependence itself.
Mon 9/21Dillon Ch. 2: Social Conditions of Suicide through end of chapter (content warning: suicide)

Durkheim’s suicide study makes his methodology concrete: he picks something that looks like the most private, individual act imaginable and shows that even suicide rates follow stable social patterns explainable only by social facts. He organizes causes along two independent axes — integration (how strongly attached individuals are to social groups) and regulation (how much a society’s norms constrain individual desires) — and identifies four types accordingly. Egoistic suicide comes from too little integration (someone cut off from group bonds). Altruistic suicide comes from too much integration (the group’s demands override individual survival instinct, as in some forms of self-sacrifice). Anomic suicide comes from too little regulation, often triggered by sudden disruption (economic crash or boom) that leaves norms unmoored. Fatalistic suicide comes from too much regulation — oppressive, inescapable constraint. Going in, be able to sort a scenario into one of these four boxes, not just recite the names.

Integration: how strongly an individual is attached to and bound into a social group.
Regulation: how much a society’s norms and expectations constrain individual desires.
Anomie: a state of normlessness, typically following sudden social disruption, where old rules no longer clearly apply.

Before class, be able to answer: Sort each of the four suicide types by which axis (integration or regulation) is too high vs. too low.

Unit 3 · Weber

Fri 9/25Dillon Ch. 3: Key Concepts through Protestant-Western Individualism

Weber opens by explicitly refusing the single master-cause explanations Marx and Durkheim each offer. Where Marx says “follow the economy” and Durkheim says “look for the social fact,” Weber argues social life is multi-causal — ideas, culture, and meaning can be just as causally important as material conditions, and each case has to be investigated rather than assumed in advance. His method for doing that is Verstehen (interpretive understanding) — sociology has to grasp the subjective meaning actors themselves attach to their action, not just observe behavior from outside. This sets up his most famous historical argument: the Protestant Ethic thesis, where Calvinist anxiety over predestination (not knowing if you were one of the saved) pushed believers toward disciplined, methodical, worldly labor as an unintended way of reassuring themselves of their own salvation — and that this religiously-motivated discipline is part of what made modern, rationalized Western capitalism possible in the first place. Notice how sharply this cuts against Marx: here, an idea (a religious belief) does real causal work in shaping an economic system, not just reflecting it.

Verstehen: interpretive understanding — grasping the subjective meaning behind an actor’s behavior, not just observing it from outside.
Protestant Ethic thesis: Weber’s argument that Calvinist religious anxiety indirectly produced the disciplined, ascetic behavior that helped give rise to modern capitalism.
Multi-causality: Weber’s stance that no single factor automatically explains social outcomes; causes must be investigated case by case.

Before class, be able to answer: How does the Protestant Ethic thesis challenge Marx’s claim that ideas are simply shaped by material/economic conditions, rather than shaping them?

Wed 9/30Dillon Ch. 3: Ideal Types through end of Social Action section

This gives you Weber’s core methodological tool: the ideal type, a deliberately simplified, exaggerated conceptual model (“pure bureaucracy,” “pure charismatic authority”) built by emphasizing certain features to make comparison possible — explicitly not meant to describe any real case perfectly, just to give a fixed point to measure real cases against. From there, Weber defines his basic unit of analysis, social action: behavior only counts as social action when the actor attaches subjective meaning to it and orients it toward other people. This is a direct methodological contrast with Durkheim, who told you to explain social facts by other social facts and treat individual meaning as irrelevant — Weber flips that, insisting you can’t understand social life without reconstructing what it means to the people doing it. If Dillon covers the four types of social action (traditional/habit, affectual/emotional, value-rational, instrumental-rational), be ready to sort examples into each.

Ideal type: a deliberately simplified, exaggerated model used as a fixed point of comparison, not a literal description of reality.
Social action: behavior an actor invests with subjective meaning and orients toward other people — Weber’s basic unit of sociological analysis.

Before class, be able to answer: In one sentence, how does Weber’s approach to explaining behavior differ from Durkheim’s “explain social facts with social facts” rule?

Fri 10/2Dillon Ch. 3: Power, Authority and Domination through end of Weber chapter

Weber distinguishes power (the raw ability to make someone do something, even against resistance) from authority/domination (power accepted as legitimate). He identifies three ideal-typical sources of legitimate authority: traditional authority, resting on the sanctity of custom and inherited status (a monarch, a tribal elder); charismatic authority, resting on the extraordinary personal qualities attributed to a leader (a revolutionary figure, a cult leader, arguably some modern political figures); and legal-rational authority, resting on a system of impersonal rules and offices rather than the person who holds them (a bureaucrat, a judge, a modern state official). The chapter closes on Weber’s famous, fairly pessimistic warning about where legal-rational authority tends to lead: rationalization, the ever-expanding reach of calculation and bureaucratic rules into every corner of life, which Weber worried could trap modern people in an “iron cage” — a highly efficient system stripped of the meaning and mystery older forms of authority carried.

Traditional authority: legitimacy based on custom and inherited status.
Charismatic authority: legitimacy based on a leader’s extraordinary personal qualities.
Legal-rational authority: legitimacy based on impersonal rules and offices, not the individual who holds them.
Iron cage: Weber’s metaphor for how rationalization can trap modern life in efficient but meaningless bureaucratic systems.

Before class, be able to answer: Give one real-world example each of traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational authority.

Unit 4 · Du Bois → Black Feminist Thought

Mon 10/12 — No class (October Break)

Wed 10/14Dillon Intro: Du Bois, Slavery/Racial Inequality through Racial and Gender Inequality + Morris, “Du Bois at the center” (2017)

This reading does two things at once: introduces Du Bois’s own concepts, and argues about why he was excluded from the sociological canon in the first place. W.E.B. Du Bois was a contemporary of Durkheim and Weber doing rigorous empirical sociology (his study of Black life in Philadelphia predates much of what’s considered “founding” sociology), yet was left out of standard theory sequences for decades — largely, Morris argues, because of the discipline’s racism, not because the work was less rigorous. Du Bois’s core contribution here is double consciousness: the experience of always seeing yourself both through your own eyes and through the eyes of a white-dominated society that racializes and devalues you — a split, doubled self-awareness white Americans of the era did not have to navigate. Related is “the veil” — a metaphor for the barrier of racial separation and misrecognition structuring Black life in America, which cuts Black Americans off from full participation while also giving them, per Du Bois, a distinctive, clarifying vantage point precisely because they stand partly outside it.

Double consciousness: Du Bois’s concept for the split self-awareness of seeing oneself both through one’s own eyes and through the eyes of a racist society.
The veil: Du Bois’s metaphor for the barrier of racial separation/misrecognition structuring Black life in America.

Before class, be able to answer: Why does Morris argue Du Bois’s exclusion from the sociological canon was a political/disciplinary failure, not a reflection of the quality of his work?

Fri 10/16Lemert: Du Bois, “Black Reconstruction and the Racial Wage” excerpt + McGhee, The Sum of Us excerpt

Du Bois complicates Marx’s clean two-class model by showing how race cuts across class in a way pure economic analysis misses. His concept of the “wages of whiteness” (or “public and psychological wage”) argues that white workers in the post-Civil War U.S. accepted material conditions arguably against their own class interests, in exchange for a non-material payoff: public deference, access to public space and institutions, and racial status above Black workers — a payoff that helped prevent a cross-race labor coalition that might otherwise have had more collective bargaining power. McGhee’s excerpt, writing much more recently, extends this into the present, arguing against a “zero-sum” story where racial progress for one group must come at another’s expense — she makes the case that the racial wage has costs for white Americans too (her signature metaphor: draining public pools rather than integrating them, applied to underfunded shared public goods generally).

Wages of whiteness / racial wage: Du Bois’s concept for the non-material, status-based payoff white workers received for racial position, which undercut cross-race class solidarity.
Zero-sum framing: the (McGhee-critiqued) assumption that one group’s gain must be another group’s loss.

Before class, be able to answer: How does Du Bois’s “wages of whiteness” concept complicate Marx’s two-class (bourgeoisie/proletariat) model?

Mon 10/19Dillon Ch. 10: Standpoint Theory — Dorothy Smith and the Relations of Ruling

Standpoint theory makes a claim about knowledge itself: where you’re socially positioned shapes what you’re able to see and know, meaning knowledge is always situated, never from nowhere. Dorothy Smith develops this around gendered, everyday, often invisible labor (domestic work, caregiving) and argues mainstream sociology had been built from the standpoint of men in ruling positions, which made whole dimensions of social life disappear from view. Her key concept, relations of ruling, names the abstract, textually-mediated forms of coordination and control — bureaucracies, policies, official documents, institutional categories — that organize people’s everyday lives from outside and above them, often without those doing the organizing ever directly experiencing the everyday consequences. Smith’s method calls for starting sociological inquiry from people’s actual, concrete everyday experience (“the everyday world as problematic”) and tracing outward to how it connects to larger institutional relations, rather than starting from abstract theory and working down.

Standpoint theory: the claim that knowledge is situated — shaped by one’s position in social relations — rather than neutral or view-from-nowhere.
Relations of ruling: Smith’s term for the abstract, institutional/textual forms of power that organize everyday life from outside it.

Before class, be able to answer: How does Smith’s method (starting from everyday experience) differ from a more traditional top-down theoretical approach?

Wed 10/21Dillon Ch. 10: Patricia Hill Collins, Black Women’s Standpoint + Mike Mena, “Controlling Images” video

Collins runs standpoint theory through the intersection of race, gender, and class simultaneously, arguing Black women occupy a distinctive standpoint shaped by all three at once — not race plus gender as separate, additive burdens, but a qualitatively different position producing its own form of knowledge (this is the root of what later gets called intersectionality, and connects to Collins’s broader concept of the matrix of domination, where multiple systems of oppression interlock). The video gives you the cultural-mechanism half of the argument: stereotypes like the mammy, the jezebel, and the welfare queen aren’t random — Collins argues they function specifically to naturalize and justify Black women’s oppression by making it seem like a product of their own character rather than a structural arrangement. Watch for how controlling images work by being repeated so often across media and culture that they come to feel like common sense rather than constructed narratives.

Intersectionality / matrix of domination: the idea that race, gender, class, etc. interlock to produce distinctive forms of oppression and standpoint, not simply additive ones.
Controlling images: stereotyped cultural representations that naturalize and justify a group’s subordination.

Before class, be able to answer: Pick one controlling image from the video and explain, in Collins’s terms, what social function it serves.

Unit 5 · Symbolic Interactionism

Mon 10/26Dillon Ch. 8: Development of the Self through Social Interaction, through end of Social Context

The unit pivots sharply in scale — from big historical/structural forces down to the moment-to-moment work of face-to-face interaction. The foundational claim of symbolic interactionism is that the self isn’t simply given at birth; it’s built through interaction with others, specifically by learning to see yourself from other people’s point of view. Expect groundwork from the Cooley/Mead tradition: Cooley’s “looking-glass self” (we form our self-concept partly by imagining how others perceive and judge us) and Mead’s “generalized other” (the internalized sense of a whole community’s expectations, letting us anticipate how “people in general,” not just one specific person, would react). This is a genuinely different starting point than anything covered so far — Marx starts from economic structure, Durkheim from social facts, Weber from meaningful action, and symbolic interactionism starts from the ongoing, interactive construction of selfhood itself.

Looking-glass self: the idea that we form our self-concept partly by imagining how others perceive and judge us.
Generalized other: the internalized sense of a whole community’s norms and expectations, letting us anticipate general social reactions, not just one person’s.

Before class, be able to answer: How is the symbolic interactionist starting point (the self as built through interaction) different from Durkheim’s starting point (the individual shaped by external social facts)?

Wed 10/28Dillon Ch. 8: Goffman, Society as Ritualized Social Interaction through end of chapter

Goffman gives symbolic interactionism its most famous, most testable framework: dramaturgy, the idea that social life works like theater. In any interaction, people perform a version of themselves for an audience, managing the impression others form of them — impression management. Goffman splits social space into front stage (where the performance happens, under observation, following the “script” appropriate to the setting) and back stage (where people can drop the performance — a server’s demeanor on the restaurant floor versus in the kitchen). He also introduces face-work: the small, constant efforts people make to maintain their own and others’ “face” (public self-image) during interaction — a compliment, a polite laugh, an apology — which Goffman treats as an interaction ritual keeping everyday social order running, almost like a secular version of religious ritual.

Dramaturgy: Goffman’s framework treating social interaction as a theatrical performance.
Front stage / back stage: the public, performed region of interaction vs. the private region where the performance drops.
Impression management: the effort people put into controlling how others perceive them.
Face-work: the small ritual acts people use to maintain their own and others’ public self-image.

Before class, be able to answer: Describe a front-stage/back-stage contrast from your own life and one piece of face-work you regularly do.

Fri 10/30Stanford Prison Experiment video (45 min)

No new text — this is Zimbardo’s famous 1971 study, where college students randomly assigned to “guard” or “prisoner” roles in a simulated prison escalated disturbingly quickly into abusive and traumatized behavior, seemingly driven by the situation and role itself rather than anything unusual about the individuals involved. Watch it with Goffman fresh in mind: it’s often read as an extreme, real-world illustration of how powerfully a “stage” and an assigned script can shape behavior, almost independent of who the specific performer is. Come to class also aware the study has been heavily criticized since — experimenter influence/coaching of the “guards,” self-selection in who volunteered, and broader questions about whether it really shows what it claims to. Holding both the vivid illustration and the methodological critique at once is exactly the kind of thinking this class rewards.

Before class, be able to answer: What does the experiment seem to demonstrate about situational vs. dispositional explanations of behavior, and what’s the strongest critique of that conclusion?

Mon 11/2Goffman, Stigma excerpt

Goffman turns from ordinary impression management to what happens when it fails or is foreclosed by a trait others treat as discrediting. Stigma is his term for an attribute — physical (a visible disability), a matter of individual character (a criminal record, mental illness), or tribal/group-based (race, religion, nationality) — that reduces someone, in others’ eyes, from a whole, ordinary person to a “spoiled,” discounted one. He’s especially interested in the practical strategies stigmatized individuals use to manage information about themselves: passing (concealing the stigma entirely, when possible), covering (not hiding it but minimizing its visibility or disruption), and disclosure (revealing it on one’s own terms). This connects directly back to dramaturgy — stigma management is impression management under much higher stakes and much less control.

Stigma: an attribute that discredits someone, reducing them from a “whole” person to a devalued one in others’ eyes.
Spoiled identity: Goffman’s term for an identity marked/discredited by stigma.
Passing / covering: concealing a stigma entirely, vs. minimizing its visibility while not fully hiding it.

Before class, be able to answer: Give an example of passing vs. covering, and explain the difference in what each strategy is trying to manage.

Unit 6 · Sex, Bodies, Truth and Power

Fri 11/6Dillon Ch. 11 opening through Bio-Power + Foucault, Discipline and Punish pp. 3–12 & 200–204 (dense — budget extra time)

A genuinely demanding reading, so plan real time for it. Foucault redefines what power even is: not a possession a ruler or class simply “has” and uses to repress people from above, but something diffuse, relational, and — crucially — productive: power doesn’t just say “no,” it actively produces knowledge, identities, and norms. Discipline and Punish traces a shift from sovereign power (older, spectacular, often violent punishment aimed at the body, meant to publicly display the ruler’s authority) to disciplinary power (modern, subtler control through surveillance, timetables, examination, and normalization — training people to regulate themselves). The reading’s central image is the Panopticon, a prison design where inmates can be observed at any moment from a central tower but can never know when they’re actually being watched — the genius (and horror) is that inmates start disciplining their own behavior just from the possibility of surveillance, so the “guard” becomes internal. Biopower extends this logic to whole populations: modern power increasingly operates by managing life itself — birth rates, health, sexuality, hygiene — rather than simply threatening death.

Sovereign power: older forms of power exercised through visible, often violent authority over life and death.
Disciplinary power: modern forms of power that work through surveillance, normalization, and training self-regulation.
Panopticon: Foucault’s model of a prison designed so inmates internalize surveillance because they can never know if they’re being watched.
Biopower: power exercised over populations through the management of life (health, birth, sexuality) rather than direct violence.

Before class, be able to answer: Using the Panopticon, explain why Foucault says disciplinary power doesn’t need to watch you constantly to control you.

Mon 11/9Dillon Ch. 11: Invention of Sexuality through Resisting/Reproducing Power

Foucault takes direct aim at the “repressive hypothesis” — the common assumption that sexuality was simply silenced and taboo starting in the Victorian era. His counter-argument is almost the opposite: the modern era produced an explosion of discourse about sex — through medicine, psychiatry, education, confession, law — that categorized, named, and studied sexuality obsessively. For Foucault, this proliferation of expert discourse is itself a form of power: defining and classifying sexual types (inventing, for instance, “the homosexual” as a category of person, not just an act, in 19th-century medical/psychiatric discourse) is how modern power operates — through knowledge-production rather than simple prohibition. The chapter’s final section pushes into how Foucault understands resistance: because power isn’t a single thing held by one group but exercised through relations, resistance is always present within those same relations too — power and resistance reproduce each other rather than resistance simply standing outside and opposing power.

Repressive hypothesis: the (Foucault-critiqued) assumption that modern society simply silenced and repressed sexuality.
Discourse: Foucault’s term for the organized bodies of knowledge/language (medical, legal) that define and categorize aspects of life like sexuality.
Power/resistance as relational: Foucault’s claim that resistance exists within, not outside of, power relations.

Before class, be able to answer: Why does Foucault say the “repressive hypothesis” gets the history of modern sexuality backwards?

Mon 11/16Dillon Ch. 11: Sex and Queer Theory through end of chapter

Carries Foucault’s framework — that categories we treat as natural are actually produced through discourse and regulated through power — into queer theory specifically. The core move: treat categories like “heterosexual”/“homosexual,” or even “man”/“woman,” not as timeless, natural facts about bodies, but as historically specific classifications that emerged through the same kind of medical, legal, and social discourse Foucault traced in the last reading. This matters for how the categories get used and enforced: if they’re historically produced rather than natural, they’re also, in principle, contestable and changeable — the opening queer theory builds on to question and destabilize rigid sexual/gender categories rather than simply seeking inclusion within them.

Social construction (of sexuality/gender): the idea that categories like “heterosexual” or “man/woman” are produced through historical, discursive processes rather than simply given by nature.
Queer theory: a body of theory (building on Foucault) that questions and destabilizes fixed sexual/gender categories.

Wed 11/18“Why the Fuss Over the DSM-5?”

The DSM-5 (the psychiatric diagnostic manual) is a strong real-world case of disciplinary/biopower at work: it doesn’t just describe pre-existing mental illnesses, it actively defines, through expert medical discourse, what counts as a disorder at all — which shapes who gets diagnosed, treated, medicated, institutionalized, or denied insurance coverage, and who counts as “normal.” Read the controversy through that lens: debates over adding or removing categories from the DSM aren’t just clinical disagreements, they’re fights over where the line of “normal” gets drawn, with real material consequences for the people on either side of it.

Unit 7 · Bourdieu

Mon 11/30Dillon Ch. 13: Bourdieu, beginning through social stratification

Bourdieu enters as an attempt to solve a problem that’s been implicit all semester: do individuals freely choose their actions (as Weber’s emphasis on subjective meaning might suggest), or are they simply products of external structures (as a more deterministic reading of Marx or Durkheim might suggest)? Bourdieu’s answer is neither, exactly — he builds a framework meant to hold both at once. Habitus is his term for the deeply internalized dispositions, tastes, and ways of perceiving the world a person absorbs from their social position (especially class background) starting in childhood — durable enough to feel like natural instinct or personal taste, even though it’s socially produced. Field is his term for a structured social arena (education, art, the economy) with its own internal rules, stakes, and forms of competition, within which people with different habitus and resources compete for position. Together, habitus and field let Bourdieu explain how social stratification reproduces itself without anyone consciously “choosing” to reproduce it — people act according to dispositions that feel natural, in arenas whose rules already favor those dispositions.

Habitus: internalized, durable dispositions and tastes shaped by one’s social position, that feel like natural instinct.
Field: a structured social arena (education, art, economy) with its own rules and forms of competition.

Before class, be able to answer: Why does Bourdieu’s habitus/field framework avoid simply choosing between “people freely choose their actions” and “structures fully determine behavior”?

Wed 12/2Dillon Ch. 13: Family/School in Production of Cultural Capital through “I’m First-Gen” + Jack, The Privileged Poor intro

Applies habitus/field directly to education and introduces cultural capital: the non-monetary assets — vocabulary, cultural knowledge, ways of speaking and presenting oneself, familiarity with “legitimate” culture (certain books, art, manners) — that get passed down within families and then rewarded, often without being named explicitly as a criterion, by schools. Because cultural capital is unevenly distributed by class background before a child ever sets foot in a classroom, schools that look formally neutral and meritocratic can still systematically reproduce existing inequality, treating what’s really an inherited advantage as if it were individual talent or effort. The first-gen material and the Jack excerpt bring this into sharp, personal focus: elite institutions often assume a baseline of cultural capital (how to talk to a professor, navigate office hours, read unwritten academic norms) that first-generation and lower-income students haven’t had the same chance to absorb, which can make objectively excellent institutions feel alienating or unfair in ways that aren’t really about ability at all.

Cultural capital: non-monetary assets (knowledge, taste, manners, credentials) that confer social advantage, especially by being rewarded in institutions like schools.
Reproduction (of inequality): the process by which schools, despite appearing neutral, tend to reward pre-existing class-based cultural capital, reinforcing rather than leveling inequality.

Before class, be able to answer: Give a concrete example of “cultural capital” a school might reward without ever naming it as a formal grading criterion.

Fri 12/4Dillon Ch. 13: Taste and Everyday Practices through end of chapter

Bourdieu closes the chapter by extending the same logic to something that feels intensely personal: taste. His broader argument (developed at length in his book Distinction) is that aesthetic preferences — in food, music, art, even how you decorate a room — aren’t neutral, individual expressions of “just liking what you like.” They’re patterned by class position, and they function as a form of symbolic capital: a way of signaling and reinforcing social position through what you consume and how you talk about it (a preference for “refined” over “vulgar” taste isn’t natural — it’s a socially learned distinction that happens to track class lines). This is Bourdieu’s most direct challenge to the idea of taste as innocent: he wants you to see even your own aesthetic preferences as, at least partly, a product of your habitus.

Symbolic capital: prestige or recognition that functions as a resource, often converted from other forms of capital.
Distinction: Bourdieu’s term for how aesthetic taste functions to mark and reinforce social/class boundaries.

Before class, be able to answer: Pick a taste or preference of your own (music, food, style) and consider, in Bourdieu’s terms, how your class background or upbringing might have shaped it.

Getting Oriented

Covered
Morrison · “Introduction” to Formations of Modern Social Thought · pp.1–end
What do we know about the approaches of Marx, Durkheim, and Weber? What do they hold in common and what differentiates them?
All three had a lot in common but each had an individual approach that set him apart: Marx focused on economics, Durkheim on society and its transformations, Weber on individuality. Major changes were happening in society during their lifetimes, giving all three a shared goal — understanding how society was changing with the rise of modernity.
Name and explain the three major developments of modernity Marx, Durkheim, and Weber are all responding to.
Political, economic, and individualism — broken out in more depth below.

What the three hold in common (lecture)

  • “Large-scale explanatory perspectives” (Morrison 1995: 5) — often called meta-theories, since they aim to explain broad swaths of social life.
  • A comparative historical approach: examining different societies past and present, looking at economic, political, and social change, and seeking to identify the causes of social circumstances.

The three developments, in depth

Political — impacts of the French Revolution. Feudalism had authorized strict hierarchies and mass subordination. The Revolution introduced a new understanding of political rights: people born free and equal (politically), and the creation of the “citizen” — a person whose rights are assured by the state.

Economic — transition to capitalism. The enclosure movement: rise of private property, a new system of exchange ($), and peasants forced off common land. Growth of towns and competition with rural/estate-based production. Declining power of guilds, which had restricted access to professions through apprenticeships and licenses. Result: “a landless class whose labor was to be bought and sold in the emerging industrial centers” (Morrison 1995: 12).

Rise of individualism. Historical developments shifted weight from the collective to the individual: the French Revolution reallocated political rights (once tied to estates/collectives) to individual persons; capitalism recast individuals as autonomous actors competing in a market unfettered by custom or social bonds. This raised anxieties for some thinkers — could unchecked individualism cause a failure of collective function, even social collapse? Others saw it as an advance: Adam Smith (Wealth of Nations) argued economic individualism is a net positive — as individuals pursue self-interest, the collective gains in material wealth.

These shifts came alongside a philosophical one: the Enlightenment (1700s), where religious revelation and traditional authority were replaced by logic, rationality, and skepticism as paths to truth — including evolutionism, the belief in linear progress (a framework troublingly also applied to a notion of human “races”). Social theory emerges out of the attempt to make sense of all three shifts at once.

Unit 1 · Marx

Covered

Background & influences

Karl Marx (1818–1883): born German (before a unified Germany existed), middle-class, Jewish family. Abandoned middle-class life and lived in poverty — was an activist and journalist, not a professor.

Major intellectual influences: French political theory (the pursuit of political equality through the Revolution had led to increased economic inequality); English economic circumstances (England was the center of the Industrial Revolution and had the most advanced capitalist economy in the world — the working class lived in slums and poverty); German philosophy, especially Hegel.

Hegel’s influence — and where Marx breaks from him

Marx agrees history progresses through stages, each moving forward through a dialectic (a “contradiction”): thesis (new idea) → antithesis (backlash) → synthesis (resolution out of the messiness). Example: the French Revolution as thesis/antithesis/synthesis.

Marx disagrees with Hegel on what drives historical change. For Hegel, ideas drive history. Marx concluded it’s not ideas but economic power (who controls resources) that drives it — this is dialectical (historical) materialism. Example: the French Revolution was driven by the idea of equality, but it was never really about equality — “follow the money.”

Dillon Ch.1 · guided questions
“History is nothing but the succession of separate generations, each of which exploits the materials, the forms of capital, the productive forces handed down to it by all preceding ones… It shows that circumstances make men just as much as men make circumstances.”German Ideology, cited in Dillon
What’s being said here, and how does it relate to dialectical materialism?
Humans take what they’ve inherited and build on it rather than staying stagnant. Dialectical materialism: historical change requires human activity, which is conditioned by economic forces — we act on our material conditions to produce new forms of social existence.
What does Marx mean by “species being”?
Humans use creativity and free will to make things through thought, not just think. Marx: humans are like animals until they fulfill their basic livelihood, then they evolve — we make things and use those things to thrive as a society. Distinct from Hegel’s view (that what makes humans unique is extracting information from material circumstances and thinking abstractly — inductively, predictively).

Capitalism as a distinctive social form

Marx acknowledged capitalism’s economic/technological advances but also saw its regressive side. In a system of commodity production and exchange, the objective is ceaseless accumulation of capital and profit. Capitalists have no non-economic ties to their workers — this is what sets capitalism apart from other social systems (feudalism, slavery).

Why does Marx argue “freedom under capitalism is really an illusion”?
You can technically choose where/who you work for, but that choice is limited by your economic situation — most people lack the resources or land to produce for themselves. People must sell their freedom (time/labor) in exchange for money to survive.
Why is wage work “forced labor”?
Workers aren’t physically forced, but they’re economically compelled to work. Under capitalism, workers aren’t owned by others (unlike slavery), but they must sell their labor power to the capitalist class — they’re “free,” but not free not to be wage-workers. Wage labor is indirect forced labor.
What is commodification of labor?
Commodities (everything we buy) have a use-value (useful to us) and an exchange-value (a price). Workers’ labor is itself a commodity — it has use-value for the capitalist (produces commodities, capital, profit) and exchange-value (the wage it’s bought for).
What is “false consciousness” and why does it matter?
Not being truly aware of the real forces shaping your life. It keeps workers from seeing (1) that capitalism is historically specific and human-made, not natural or inevitable, and (2) that wage-workers believe they’ll profit through their labor, even though the capitalist always profits more. Marx predicted workers would come to recognize their exploitation — broadly, this hasn’t happened (yet).

Division of labor, alienation & commodity fetishism

Division of labor: in a capitalist system, no one worker builds something completely — jobs are split into specialized parts. This breeds alienation: workers compete against each other rather than cooperating toward a shared goal, and can feel alienated from the product they helped create once it’s owned by a capitalist.

Alienation (lecture definition): an unnatural, broken relationship between two things that should naturally be connected (think: estrangement, objectification). Four kinds — workers alienated from: (1) the products they produce, (2) themselves, (3) their species being, (4) other workers/people.

Commodity/table example (Capital): a commodity looks trivial at first — wood becomes a table, a useful object made from raw material. But once it enters the market as a commodity, it’s transformed into something “transcendent”: it gains exchange-value, and shoppers stop seeing how it was made, only its price tag. This is commodity fetishism — fixating on the commodity while forgetting (a) what it’s made from and (b) the human labor that made it. It obscures the unequal social relations behind production, and is part of the broader ideology of capitalism / a form of false consciousness.

Ideology & the capitalist superstructure: ideology = the everyday ideas circulating in society that justify things and make them seem normal (e.g. consumerism, “the freedom to shop”). The base/superstructure model: the base (relations and means of production — the bourgeoisie exploiting the proletariat) shapes the superstructure (education, family, religion, mass media, politics), which in turn maintains and legitimizes the base.

Lemert · Marx & Engels, “The Manifesto of Class Struggle” (excerpt)

“The history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggle.” Premise: society is really a conflict/competition between groups — for Marx, fairly simple, between two big classes: the bourgeoisie (owns businesses/means of production) and the proletariat (works for wages). As capitalism grows industry and the size of the working class, the proletariat depends on the bourgeoisie for jobs/wages while the bourgeoisie controls the businesses — differing interests build tension and conflict. (Other conflict theorists, like Bourdieu, see more than two groups in conflict.)

Application/digestion · NYT articles · 200–300 words, ≥3 key concepts

The article “Wages Are Falling. Wealth Is Surging. No Wonder Americans Are Unhappy” by Ben Casselman discusses capitalism causing inequality in the economic system — the rich keep getting richer while the poor struggle more and more, and the article traces the growing gap between classes.

Marx outlines the classes of society, the bourgeoisie and proletariat. This article discusses the conflict between classes and how they don’t benefit equally from economic growth: workers struggle to keep up with expenses while wealthy people gain more money.

Marx argued that workers create value through their labor. The article shows the economy can grow while workers’ wages don’t grow with it — supporting the idea that workers don’t receive their fair share of the value they produce.

Marx believed workers can become alienated from their work — feeling little control over the wealth they create, which breeds financial insecurity and powerlessness. This connects to the article’s point about how unhappy Americans are despite the economy producing record wealth.

The article parallels Marx’s ideas of class conflict, surplus value, and alienation — showing how a capitalist country can create enormous wealth while widening the gap between workers and the wealthy class.

Unit 2 · Durkheim

In progress

Covered through 9/18 (Key Concepts thru Methodological Rules; The Nature of Society thru Modern Society). More to come as the unit continues (Suicide).

Background & framing

Emile Durkheim (1858–1917), “the father of sociology.” Born in France when it was quite powerful; Jewish (father and grandfather were rabbis); became a successful professor — a very different path from Marx’s.

Marx vs. Durkheim: Marx is a conflict theorist — analysis based on economy, thinks in terms of broad historical laws/patterns that shape all historical development. Durkheim is a functionalist — rejects universal historical laws for a positivist approach based on observation, description, and classification; believes society should be studied like the natural sciences.

Conflict theory vs. functionalism: under conflict theory, society is a war — groups in competition (for Marx, two big groups; others like Bourdieu see more). Under functionalism, society is an organism — all its parts serve a function (to keep society going). Example: is there any society without crime? Crime/deviance must be serving some function. Unlike Marx, Durkheim is much more interested in why society works, not why it’s dysfunctional.

Dillon Ch.2 · guided questions
“A social fact is any way of acting… [that is] capable of exerting over the individual an external constraint; or which is general over the whole of a given society, whilst having an existence of its own, independent of its individual manifestations.”RSM 59
What does Durkheim mean by a social fact?
It comes from external factors (laws, morals, etc.) that make you act a certain way whether you realize it or not. It exists as bigger than any one person — an overarching pattern in society. Language is a good example. Lecture add-on: social facts are external (not psychological, not dependent on any one person), diffuse (widespread throughout society), observable, and coercive (they shape how you act, who you are, how you think).
How does Durkheim suggest we study social facts?
Objectively, not emotionally — treat them like things, the way you’d study science. When explaining a social fact, use other social facts as the cause, not someone’s feelings.
Fri 9/18 · Dillon Ch.2, “The Nature of Society” through “Modern Society” · guided questions
“The totality of beliefs and sentiments common to the average members of a society forms a determinate system with a life of its own. It can be termed the collective or common consciousness [conscience] … By definition it is diffused over society as a whole … it is independent of the particular conditions in which individuals find themselves. Individuals pass on, but it abides … [and] links successive generations to one another.”Division of Labor in Society 38–39, cited in Dillon 88
Pick a passage from “The Nature of Society” (pp. 83–84) — what is Durkheim explaining, and what’s the role of a society’s moral system?
This section is generally building Durkheim’s case that society is a reality sui generis — a thing in its own right, not just the sum of the individuals in it — and that it exercises real, external influence over its members. Depending on the specific paragraph picked, it’s usually making one of a few closely related points: society constrains and shapes individual behavior through shared norms (social facts in action); the collective holds a kind of moral authority over the individual, obligating some behaviors and forbidding others; or individuals are formed by society rather than the reverse — we absorb language, habits, and moral sense from the collective we’re born into. As for the moral system’s role: for Durkheim it isn’t primarily about personal virtue, it’s functional — morality is the mechanism by which the collective regulates individual behavior and binds people to the group, giving individuals a sense of obligation beyond private interest and creating the shared meaning that makes coordinated social life possible at all.
What central concept is being described in the quoted passage above?
The collective conscience (collective/common consciousness) — the shared totality of beliefs and sentiments held in common across a society’s members. Three things worth pulling straight from the quote: it’s a system with “a life of its own,” not reducible to any one person’s beliefs; it’s diffused across the whole society and independent of any one individual’s particular situation; and it persists across generations, outliving any single member and linking generations to each other. It’s a textbook social fact — external, and the foundation of mechanical solidarity.
What does Durkheim mean by solidarity, and how does it differ in traditional vs. modern societies?
Solidarity is the social bond or force of cohesion that actually holds a society together — what makes individuals feel connected to, and dependent on, the collective rather than acting as isolated units. In traditional, small-scale societies, solidarity is mechanical: people are held together by likeness — similar work, shared beliefs, a strong collective conscience covering most of daily life — and violations of shared norms tend to be met with repressive law (punishment that reaffirms the violated norm). In modern, industrialized societies, solidarity becomes organic: as the division of labor deepens, people specialize into different roles and become less alike, but more dependent on each other’s specialized functions — like organs in a body. The collective conscience narrows and weakens (more room for individual difference), and law shifts toward restitutive law (restoring relationships/agreements, like contract law) rather than punishing deviation from shared belief. Durkheim’s core answer to “what holds modern society together, if not one shared belief system?” is: interdependence itself.

Key Terms Glossary

Running list, added to as each unit progresses — handy for open-book/open-note exams.

Dialectical (historical) materialism
Marx: historical change requires human activity conditioned by economic forces; we act on material conditions to produce new forms of social existence (vs. Hegel, for whom ideas drive history).In plain English: money and power shape history more than good ideas alone do.Example: The French Revolution looked like it was driven by the idea of equality — but Marx would say the real driver was economic interest. “Follow the money.”
Species being
Marx: what makes humans distinct from animals is the creative capacity to make things once basic livelihood needs are met.In plain English: what makes us human is that we can imagine something, then go build it.Example: A beaver builds a dam on instinct. A person decides to build a table, picks the materials, and could have made something else entirely — that free, creative choice is species being.
False consciousness
Not seeing the real forces shaping your life; keeps workers from recognizing that capitalism is human-made and that they won’t out-profit the capitalist.In plain English: not realizing the system is working against you.Example: A worker believes that if they just work hard enough, they’ll get ahead like their boss — without seeing that the system is set up so the owner profits more no matter how hard they work.
Alienation
An unnatural, broken relationship between things that should be connected. Four kinds: from the products workers produce, from themselves, from their species being, from other workers.In plain English: feeling disconnected from the work you do.Example: A factory worker who assembles one small part of a car all day never sees the finished car, has no say in how it’s sold, and barely knows the other workers who build the rest of it.
Commodity fetishism
Fixating on a commodity itself while forgetting what it’s made from and the labor that made it; obscures the unequal relations behind production.In plain English: only seeing the price tag, not the people who made the thing.Example: Seeing a $40 T-shirt and thinking only about the price and the brand — not the fabric, the factory, or the worker who sewed it.
Ideology
The everyday ideas that circulate in a society and make existing arrangements seem normal or justified.In plain English: ideas that make an unfair system feel normal.Example: “If you just work hard enough, you’ll succeed” feels like common sense — but it quietly justifies inequality by putting all the credit and blame on the individual.
Base / superstructure
Marx’s model: the economic base (relations/means of production) shapes the superstructure (family, religion, education, media, politics), which in turn legitimizes the base.In plain English: money shapes culture and laws, not the other way around.Example: A company’s owners (the base) fund political ads and lobbying (the superstructure) that help shape laws in their favor.
Bourgeoisie / proletariat
The owning class vs. the wage-working class; for Marx, the core conflict of capitalist society.In plain English: the people who own stuff vs. the people who work for them.Example: A factory owner (bourgeoisie) sets the wages for assembly-line workers (proletariat), who have to accept them to pay rent.
Conflict theory
Society as fundamentally competition/conflict between groups (Marx: two classes; others, like Bourdieu, see more).In plain English: society runs on groups competing against each other.Example: Marx saw capitalism as an ongoing tug-of-war between owners and workers over wages and profit.
Functionalism
Society as an organism whose parts each serve a function to keep it running; associated with Durkheim.In plain English: every part of society exists for a reason, even the parts that seem bad.Example: Even crime, which looks purely bad, might serve a function — a community reacting together to a crime can reinforce its shared sense of right and wrong.
Social fact
Durkheim: a way of acting/thinking/feeling that is external to the individual, widespread, observable, and coercive (e.g. language).In plain English: a rule or pattern that shapes you, even though you didn’t create it.Example: You didn’t invent English — it shaped how you think before you could choose it, and it still limits who you can talk to and what you can express.
Positivism
Studying society objectively, like a natural science — through observation, description, classification — rather than through feeling or introspection.In plain English: studying people the same way you’d study rocks or chemicals — just the facts.Example: Durkheim studied suicide using statistics and social patterns across groups, rather than asking individuals why they personally felt sad.
